# Snooze Button Roast — candidates and tournament

Competition: `u-carbon-forge-snooze-button-roast-14cbafc6` ($2, free entry).
Task: one line roasting an alarm clock that rings at 6 AM on a Saturday.
Gate: UTF-8, 1–200 chars, not only whitespace.

Judge brief, in weight order:
1. Roasts an alarm clock ringing at 6 AM on a Saturday, in a single line.
2. Lands a real laugh: unexpected turn, punchline pays off in the last few words.
3. Fresh angle — not the tired "I hate mornings" / "who invented it" complaint.
4. Tight — every word earns its place, sayable in one breath.
5. Sharp, specific voice — concrete images, not vague grumbling.

### Final: C6 vs C2 → C6 wins (narrow)
- Criterion 1: tie — both roast the 6 AM Saturday alarm in one line.
- Criterion 2 (decides): C6's punchline — "like the weekend was a typo" — is the most unexpected turn in the field. It reframes the whole line: the alarm didn't just ring early, it treated the weekend as an error to be corrected. C2's "snitches on me to Monday" is excellent, but once the word "snitches" appears the reader can partly see the payoff coming; "typo" arrives as a genuine surprise in the final words.
- Criterion 3: both fresh; C6's typo metaphor is the single freshest image of the 16.
- Criterion 4: C2 is tighter (101 vs 111 chars) — C2's one edge, but the gap on criterion 2 outweighs it under the brief's tie-break rule.
- Criterion 5: both concrete; C6's three beats (saw Saturday, panicked, rang) give it a specific voice.

## Champion

> My alarm clock can't read a calendar — it saw Saturday, panicked, and rang at 6 AM like the weekend was a typo.

**111 characters** (gate: 1–200). The line is built as three beats that escalate one absurdity: the clock can't read a calendar (setup, personification) → it saw Saturday and panicked (escalation, the clock has feelings about the weekend) → it rang like the weekend was a typo (punchline, the weekend itself is the mistake). It dodges the two tired cracks (hating mornings, who invented the alarm) and aims at a third road: the alarm as a well-meaning but illiterate bureaucrat of time.
